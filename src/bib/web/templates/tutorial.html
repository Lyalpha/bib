A quick introduction to stellar population models and the explorer tools

What an SSP is, and how to read the explorers.

1 · What population synthesis does

Take one star and physics can tell you how it evolves: how bright and how hot it is at every age. A galaxy, though, contains billions of stars of different masses, born together and evolving at wildly different speeds. Population synthesis answers the practical question: if a million solar masses of gas turns into stars today, what does that population look like after 1 Myr? 100 Myr? 10 Gyr? It evolves a grid of stellar models, weights them by how many stars of each mass are born, and sums their light.

The result for a single burst of star formation is a simple stellar population (SSP): every star has the same age and initial composition, and only the stellar mass varies. SSPs are the LEGO bricks of galaxy modelling — any star-formation history can be assembled from them. Everything in the SSP Explorer is an SSP, normalised to 106 M☉ formed, tabulated at 51 ages spaced by 0.1 dex in log(age) from 106 to 1011 years.

2 · The knobs you can turn

Initial mass function (IMF)

The IMF sets how many stars of each mass are born. Massive stars are rare but dominate the ultraviolet and ionizing light; low-mass stars are numerous and lock up most of the mass. BPASS quotes the high-mass slope α of ξ(log10 M) — number of stars per decade in mass. Salpeter is α = 1.35, which is a straight line of slope −2.35 on the dN/dM plot below. A larger α means a steeper drop and fewer massive stars.

Two shapes appear in this tool. The broken power-law (BPASS default, in the spirit of Kroupa 2001) is shallower below 0.5 M☉ and Salpeter-like above. Chabrier (2003) uses a log-normal turnover at low mass and the same high-mass slope, so it has fewer faint M dwarfs but a similar budget of O and B stars. Mmax (100 or 300 M☉) only changes the rarest, hottest stars. Steeper or flatter high-mass slopes exist in the full release (α = 1.00 or 1.70). Compare them in the SSP Explorer — the UV and ionizing panels move more than the optical.

Metallicity

zXXX labels give the initial mass fraction of elements heavier than helium: z020 means Z = 0.020 (roughly solar), z002 is a tenth of that, and the grid reaches down to zem5 (Z = 10−5). Metal-poor stars are hotter and more compact, their winds are weaker, and their populations produce far more ionizing photons — a key ingredient for understanding the early Universe.

Alpha enhancement (v2.3)

Not all "metals" are alike. Core-collapse supernovae make mostly alpha elements (O, Ne, Mg, Si…), while type Ia supernovae contribute iron later. Old and rapidly formed populations are therefore alpha-enhanced: more alpha elements relative to iron than the Sun. The BPASS v2.3 release provides SEDs at five abundance mixes, Δ(log α/Fe) from −0.2 to +0.6, at the same total Z — so an alpha-rich spectrum is also an iron-poor one. Select a v2.3 population in the SSP Explorer and the alpha dropdown activates.

Single vs. binary populations

The headline BPASS feature. Most massive stars are born with a close companion, and interaction is the rule, not the exception: a star that swells into a giant can spill its envelope onto its companion (Roche-lobe overflow), be stripped down to its hot helium core, spin up or rejuvenate its partner, or plunge into a shared common envelope. Consequences you can see in this tool:

  • Stripped stars keep binary populations bluer and harder-ionizing for far longer — single-star populations fade in the UV after ~10 Myr, binary ones don't. Compare sin vs bin at fixed age in the SSP Explorer.
  • Mass gainers and mergers look younger than they are (blue stragglers).
  • Supernovae and compact-object mergers happen later and in different numbers than single-star models predict.

Dust extinction

Choosing a curve and E(B−V) extinguishes the SED. Each wavelength is multiplied by 10−0.4 Aλ, as if a dust screen sat in front of the population. That is not attenuation through a mixture of stars and dust, and the light removed from the spectrum is not reprocessed into the infrared. The four curves are CCM89 and F99 (Milky Way), Gordon 03 (SMC bar) and Calzetti 00 (starbursts), with RV where the curve allows it. Calzetti et al. published their curve as an attenuation law; here it is used the same way as the others, as a screen. Outside a curve's published wavelength range nothing is applied — the spectrum is left as it is rather than extrapolated — and the region is shaded, so you can see where the extinction stops. Photometry through such passbands is flagged in the table. At z > 0 you can also switch on IGM attenuation: the observer-frame spectrum is multiplied by the mean transmission of intervening neutral hydrogen bluewards of Lyα(1+z) (Inoue et al. 2014 or Madau 1995) — a sightline average, without a damping wing. Every magnitude states whether it is dust-extinguished, IGM-attenuated, both or unattenuated.

3 · Using the SSP Explorer

  1. Pick a population — release, single/binary, IMF, metallicity (and alpha mix for v2.3) from the sidebar dropdowns.
  2. Scrub through age with the slider, or press ▶ play and watch the blue side collapse and the red giants take over. Toggle extra panels (star counts, ionizing photons, SN rates, surviving mass, yields) — they all follow the same playbar. The speed slider covers decades of log-age per second; frames between grid ages are interpolated for smoothness (see the honesty note below).
  3. Pin what you want to keep. "Pin SSP on plots" freezes the current SED slice and overlays that population's history on every visible timeseries plot — then change any control and compare. Pin one (or more) and tick sum visible spectra to add the live SSP to every pin at the ages shown: a mix of visible spectra, not a star-formation history. Sync visible spectra ages locks every overlay to the playhead.
  4. Extinguish the SED, or rescale mass — mass is a pure vertical shift (spectra and star counts scale linearly with formed mass). Dust extinction multiplies the SED by the curve and leaves the other panels alone. Removed light is not reprocessed.
  5. Overlay filters and read magnitudes. The photometry table (collapsible, below the SED) gives AB magnitudes (absolute, i.e. at 10 pc) through the bundled filter sets for the live SED and every pinned one. Upload any two-column wavelength/transmission file to get magnitudes through your own filter.
  6. Export the SED plot as PNG, or the spectrum as CSV/JSON at full native resolution.

A note on scientific honesty

Animation frames between BPASS grid ages are linear interpolations added purely for visual smoothness — they are not model output. Every number you can read out, tabulate or download (photometry tables, CSV/JSON exports) is evaluated at true grid points only. {% if has_synthetic %}And while the synthetic demo banner is up, the loaded data are toy shapes for exploring the interface — ingest real BPASS products before drawing any conclusions.{% endif %}

4 · Units and conventions

  • SED flux: solar luminosities per angstrom (L☉/Å) for a population of 106 M☉ formed, rescalable in the UI; wavelengths in Å (1–100,000 Å at 1 Å resolution in the real data).
  • Ages: log10(age/yr) = 6.0 … 11.0 in steps of 0.1 (51 bins).
  • Magnitudes computed here: AB system, absolute (10 pc), photon-weighted for every filter. BPASS's own colour tables are in Vega magnitudes for the classic bands.
  • Metallicity Z: initial mass fraction in metals; BPASS takes Z☉ = 0.020.
