A short tour of BPASS (and of this tool)

Ten minutes of background, then hands-on with both explorers. No prior knowledge of population synthesis needed.

1 · What population synthesis does

Take one star and physics can tell you how it evolves: how bright and how hot it is at every age. A galaxy, though, contains billions of stars of different masses, born together and evolving at wildly different speeds. Population synthesis answers the practical question: if a million solar masses of gas turns into stars today, what does that population look like after 1 Myr? 100 Myr? 10 Gyr? It evolves a grid of stellar models, weights them by how many stars of each mass are born, and sums their light.

The result for a single burst of star formation is a simple stellar population (SSP): every star has the same age and initial composition, and only the stellar mass varies. SSPs are the LEGO bricks of galaxy modelling — any star-formation history can be assembled from them. Everything in the SED Explorer is an SSP, normalised to 106 M☉ formed, tabulated at 51 ages spaced by 0.1 dex in log(age) from 106 to 1011 years.

2 · The knobs you can turn

Initial mass function (IMF)

The IMF sets how many stars of each mass are born. Massive stars are rare but dominate the ultraviolet light; low-mass stars are numerous and hoard most of the mass. BPASS names its broken-power-law IMFs imfXXX_YYY, where XXX encodes the upper slope and YYY the maximum stellar mass in M☉. The default, imf135_300, uses a Salpeter-like upper slope (the "135") from 0.5 M☉ up to a 300 M☉ limit, with a shallower slope below 0.5 M☉. Variants include imf100_300 (a flatter, more top-heavy upper slope) and imf_chab300 (a Chabrier 2003 turnover at low mass). Comparing them in the SED Explorer shows how much of a population's light rides on its rarest stars.

Metallicity

zXXX labels give the initial mass fraction of elements heavier than helium: z020 means Z = 0.020 (roughly solar), z002 is a tenth of that, and the grid reaches down to zem5 (Z = 10−5). Metal-poor stars are hotter and more compact, their winds are weaker, and their populations produce far more ionizing photons — a key ingredient for understanding the early Universe.

Alpha enhancement (v2.3)

Not all "metals" are alike. Core-collapse supernovae make mostly alpha elements (O, Ne, Mg, Si…), while type Ia supernovae contribute iron later. Old and rapidly formed populations are therefore alpha-enhanced: more alpha elements relative to iron than the Sun. The BPASS v2.3 release provides SEDs at five abundance mixes, Δ(log α/Fe) from −0.2 to +0.6, at the same total Z — so an alpha-rich spectrum is also an iron-poor one. Select a v2.3 population in the SED Explorer and the alpha dropdown activates.

Single vs. binary populations

The headline BPASS feature. Most massive stars are born with a close companion, and interaction is the rule, not the exception: a star that swells into a giant can spill its envelope onto its companion (Roche-lobe overflow), be stripped down to its hot helium core, spin up or rejuvenate its partner, or plunge into a shared common envelope. Consequences you can see in this tool:

  • Stripped stars keep binary populations bluer and harder-ionizing for far longer — single-star populations fade in the UV after ~10 Myr, binary ones don't. Compare sin vs bin at fixed age in the SED Explorer.
  • Mass gainers and mergers look younger than they are (blue stragglers).
  • Supernovae and compact-object mergers happen later and in different numbers than single-star models predict.

Dust

Interstellar dust dims and reddens starlight before it reaches us. The SED Explorer can apply four attenuation/extinction laws — CCM89 and F99 (Milky Way), Gordon 03 (SMC bar) and Calzetti 00 (starbursts) — parameterised by E(B−V) and, where applicable, RV. Outside a law's validated wavelength range the curve is held at its boundary value and the affected region is flagged, so you always know when you are extrapolating.

3 · Using the SED Explorer

  1. Pick a population — release, single/binary, IMF, metallicity (and alpha mix for v2.3) from the sidebar dropdowns.
  2. Scrub through age with the slider, or press ▶ play and watch the blue side collapse and the red giants take over. The speed slider covers decades of log-age per second; frames between grid ages are interpolated for smoothness (see the honesty note below).
  3. Pin what you want to keep. "Keep this SED" freezes the current curve as a persistent trace with its own chip — recolour or remove it there — then change any control and compare.
  4. Apply dust or rescale mass — mass is a pure vertical shift (everything is linear in formed mass), dust reshapes the curve.
  5. Overlay filters and read magnitudes. The photometry table gives AB magnitudes (absolute, i.e. at 10 pc) through the bundled filter sets for the live SED and every pinned one. Upload any two-column wavelength/transmission file to get magnitudes through your own filter.
  6. Export the plot as PNG, or the spectrum as CSV/JSON at full native resolution.

4 · Using the HR Explorer

The HR Explorer leaves populations behind and follows individual stellar models — the raw material of the synthesis — across the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram (temperature vs. luminosity, astronomy's most information-dense plot).

  1. Choose a model: filter by metallicity and single/primary/secondary, then pick from the curated list. Each entry says why it is interesting.
  2. Press play. The marker traces the evolutionary track while the readout updates the star's age, mass, radius, core masses, surface composition, mass-loss rate and — for binaries — companion mass, orbital period and separation. Playback follows the model's own timesteps, so it naturally lingers where evolution is fast and interesting.
  3. Jump to events. Chips below the plot mark detected milestones — ZAMS, main-sequence turnoff, core exhaustions, giant branches, Wolf–Rayet stripping, Roche-lobe overflow, common-envelope plunges, thermal pulses. Click one to jump the clock there.
  4. Watch the binary panel. Next to the HRD, the system is drawn to a common scale: separation, stellar radii and orbital motion all track the model (orbit speed is compressed for display). The visual language:
    • Colour — effective temperature, mapped through a blackbody palette (red ≈ 3,000 K → blue-white ≳ 20,000 K).
    • Size — stellar radius, to scale with the separation.
    • Soft halo — strong stellar wind (the halo grows with the mass-loss rate).
    • Teardrop + particle stream — Roche-lobe overflow feeding the companion.
    • Translucent envelope around both stars — common-envelope evolution; watch the separation shrink dramatically.
    Single-star models show one star with the same colour/size/wind rules.
  5. Population backdrop: optionally shade the HRD with the density of an entire population at matching age — see where your one star sits among its million siblings.

A note on scientific honesty

Animation frames between BPASS grid ages or model timesteps are linear interpolations added purely for visual smoothness — they are not model output. Every number you can read out, tabulate or download (photometry tables, CSV/JSON exports) is evaluated at true grid points only. Model files also do not tabulate central abundances, so evolutionary events are detected heuristically from core masses, surface composition and track morphology; event markers are labelled accordingly. {% if has_synthetic %}And while the synthetic demo banner is up, the loaded data are toy shapes for exploring the interface — ingest real BPASS products before drawing any conclusions.{% endif %}

5 · Units and conventions

  • SED flux: solar luminosities per angstrom (L☉/Å) for a population of 106 M☉ formed, rescalable in the UI; wavelengths in Å (1–100,000 Å at 1 Å resolution in the real data).
  • Ages: log10(age/yr) = 6.0 … 11.0 in steps of 0.1 (51 bins).
  • Magnitudes computed here: AB system, absolute (10 pc), with photon-counting detectors assumed where the filter metadata says so. BPASS's own colour tables are in Vega magnitudes for the classic bands.
  • Metallicity Z: initial mass fraction in metals; BPASS takes Z☉ = 0.020.
